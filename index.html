The Golden State Turned White

The Rise and Vanishing of California’s Frozen Reservoir

Team: Aurora Cong, Pali Jain, Kitty Ming, Trang Kieu

Same mountains, eight years apart

On April 1, 2015, California's governor stood in a dry meadow in the Sierra Nevada that should have been buried in snow. Eight years later, the same mountains held one of the deepest snowpacks on record. How can one place swing that far?

Every winter, one line

Each line traces one winter: how much of the Sierra Nevada was covered in snow from November to July. Snow arrives, peaks, and melts every year, but how high it climbs and how long it lasts changes a lot. Click years below to compare them, and hover over the chart to read exact values.

Water year = October through September (the 2023 water year runs Oct 2022 – Sep 2023). Snow % is computed from MODIS daily snow cover images, combined over 4 days to see past clouds.

Prototype writeup

Current Progress:

We chose the NASA MODIS dataset and focused our project on the Sierra Nevada snowpack. We used the difference between the 2015 drought and the 2023 record snow year as our hook. We wrote a Python pipeline that downloads daily MODIS snow cover images from NASA’s GIBS API, combines four days at a time to see past clouds, and converts pixel colors back into snow or no snow using NASA’s published colormap. From those images, we calculated the percent of the Sierra Nevada covered by snow twice a month for every winter from 2013 to 2024. We also planned out the structure of our page from the opening hook to our final takeaway. For our first D3 interactive visualization, we made a line chart where each winter is one line and readers can click years to compare them, and hovering above it shows the exact snow cover values.

Predicted Challenges:

We think that the most challenging part of this project will be making the interactive winter playback map part. When readers get to this part of the article, they will be able to drag a slider and see snow build up and then melt across the Sierra during one winter. Every point on the slider is a different satellite image, so we’ll have to keep the page running pretty fast while we load lots of images. Also, to not cause confusion, we’ll probably have to explain that the image pixels are what give us the snow percentages (and that they aren’t direct measurements). We’ll also have to connect the story we found with the snow changes to the impact on the reservoirs and the downstream vegetation while still sticking to the point. And finally, we need to play around with different visualizations so that we can find the visualization through which readers can best understand our goal of showing the drier and wetter years (and better than static visualizations could show).

Data: NASA MODIS (Terra) via Worldview / GIBS. Built with D3.js for DSC 106 at UC San Diego.
